**High tech or low tech?**  
Clandestine firecracker or a dignitary’s cigar? A clunky Dodge with flowers painted on its ass or a Mégane as smooth as a cosmic suppository? A patched-up 40 MB Mac held together with scraps of metal or a Super Pentium stuffed to the gills with crackable software? Beneath this women’s-magazine-style dialectic lies a political and philosophical stake as old as civilization itself. As old as the day man first placed a leaf over his genitals and had to decide whether everyone would create their own fig leaf according to personal fantasy, or whether a certified creator of standardized leaves would be appointed for the whole community.

High tech presupposes knowledge, held by the priests of high technology. One must understand its mysteries and have the means to keep up, day after day, with its dazzling advances. The tool precedes and anticipates need. The machine constantly increases in power and complexity. Yet at any given moment it allows only a single truth, at the summit of technology, just as there is only one Mount Everest in the Himalayas. High tech creates power, a hierarchy of initiates. It belongs as much to religion as to technique— even if admission to its paradise is paid in cash.

Low tech naturalizes technology. Only prior need or desire justifies the tool. There is no such thing as a bad machine—only useless functions. The machine, diverted or reused however one likes, becomes an atheist object. A suitable tool that anyone can work over at will, as humble and noble as a fork or a stick one leans on to climb stairs more easily. Low tech distrusts hierarchies: it assumes practical knowledge, and therefore as many uses as there are users. It desacralizes the machine, now reduced to a simple domestic object.

**Low Tech Diogenes vs. High Tech Plato**

If high tech were to have a philosopher, it would be Plato.  
If low tech were to have a philosopher, it would be Diogenes.

In the 4th century BCE, the elder Plato loathed Diogenes of Sinope, a disciple of Antisthenes the Cynic. And Diogenes returned the feeling. We can therefore weave an exaggerated and subjective metaphor, while recalling one detail of philosophical Cynicism—the exact opposite of vulgar cynicism: it was bourgeois civilization, the possessive offspring of the industrial revolutions, that perverted the meaning of the word *cynicism*. By portraying Diogenes as a filthy man who masturbated in public, scorning modesty and moral rules, respectable society ignored the meaning of his example: everyone masturbates, but in secret; I, having no shame about my desires, masturbate in public—and thus, involving no technique other than my own body, I denounce hypocrisy.

Diogenes’ philosophy is expressed entirely through gesture—the exemplary act. Low tech, if not entirely no-tech, finds its happiness in the most primitive reality. Diogenes is content with little: the gifts of nature, or—if we extrapolate what a Diogenes of today might be—the waste products of high technology. All it takes is to rummage through a junkyard for an old machine and patch it back together with scraps.

Plato, on the other hand, derives the sensible world from an intelligible reality that pre-exists it in a separate realm of **Essences in capital letters**, of Ideas and pure Forms. A universe above everything—*“High”* and certainly not *“Low.”* To the musician who tentatively tunes his instrument, Plato prefers the purely cerebral scholar who discovers the mathematics of harmony. Let us wager that he would have regarded the recycling art of techno artists as a foul heresy.

Plato’s philosophy lives entirely in discourse—in the superior Idea. High tech in the sense of a perpetual quest for the best, for the most abyssal efficiency and therefore for omnipotence. But these qualities are nothing more than abstractions, for the person who acquires the latest cutting-edge machine will use, at best, only 5 to 10 percent of its capacities. More than anything, it is the *idea* of possessing this ultra-high-performance beast that distinguishes you from the common herd of mortals.

To those who reproached Diogenes for frequenting brothels assiduously, he retorted: *“The sun penetrates latrines perfectly well without being soiled.”*  
 If others tried to disgust him with venal women, he added: *“I do not find it improper to travel aboard a ship on which many others have already sailed.”*

Aside from the macho character of the comparison, the venal woman constitutes a perfect metaphor for low tech. Plato’s ideal woman pushes perfection to such a point that it becomes almost indecent to sleep with her, in the same way one does not buy the latest fashionable car in order to drive it, but to parade it and display one’s status. Woman, automobile, or computer—the object must remain an Ideal. It gets dirty and drifts closer to banality with every use. That is precisely why the pure knights of high technology never keep a computer or an automobile for more than six months.

When Plato kneels before Dionysius, tyrant **of Syracuse**, in order to better convert him to his philosophy, Diogenes mockingly questions a self-proclaimed demigod, **Alexander the Great**: *“So you are indeed that Alexander they say is a bastard?”* Plato called Diogenes a *“rabid Socrates.”* Diogenes, for his part, mocked Plato: *“But what use can a man be who has already spent all his time philosophizing without ever troubling anyone?”*

Plato’s thought shares with high-tech logorrhea the concern of always placing itself on the right side of the fence, under the fallacious pretext of making the powerful more likeable and progressive. If capitalism cherishes high tech for the dazzling jobs it is supposed to create, it is in the name of progress—this piece of smoke and mirrors—that Plato serves power.

**Portrait of the High-Tech Dog as a Mega Canigou Eater**

“Taking on MS-Windows is a bit like buying a small pet. The dog only costs 500 francs, but then you have to spend 2,500 francs to clean the carpet”… That sums up the entire high-tech logic of Microsoft’s offering, as described by Marc Dodge of Open Computing.

With every new version of Windows or Microsoft Word, the humble user is ordered to pull out their wallet immediately—or risk sinking into ridicule. A ridicule that advertising and newspapers in the pay of the multinational have made it their mission to promote.

Let us forget Monsieur Naze, insensitive to pubescent reason, who still feeds Diogenes—the wild dog he has befriended—the bony leftovers of his meals. To my left, Monsieur Ringue persists in giving nothing but plain Canigou to his dachshund Hector. Poor beast, floppy ears, **incapable of sitting pretty or barking at thieves.** To my left, Monsieur Moderne and his German shepherd Attila, devourer of Super Canigou with added protein. He barks at the homeless and does somersaults to earn his treats. But beware: as soon as protein- and vitamin-enriched Mega Canigou hits the supermarket shelves, Attila will get stomach aches—unless his master immediately switches him to Mega Canigou, a celestial gruel that grants the gift of double vision to the happy chosen canines.

First high-tech reflex: *always more.* If, on date *d*, thanks to my investments and my demands for quality, I am at the cutting edge of technology, I won’t stay there long, because at date *d \+ 1* there will always be something better. So I must re-equip myself. Second high-tech reflex: comparison with my neighbor, whom I cannot possibly imagine having better gear than I do.

Hence the primary mission of the high-tech market: to constantly create new, fantasized desires to be satisfied—manufactured needs that only those who control technological “progress” and the vast market of suckers can satisfy, in exchange for cash.

Do not believe that the internet is impermeable to the high-tech mindset. What else can you call this frantic hunt for new plug-ins and other heavy, spectacular software? My virtual neighbor splashes “Shibam\! Pow\! Blop\! Wizz\!” all over every page of his very-own website, and nothing moves on *my* very-own site? Yikes\! Come on then—I puff up and repuff my memory, and here comes Real Audio, NetShow, QuickTime VR, Real Video, Adobe Premiere, NetMeeting, Applet FX, JetEffects, Flash, Flash 2, SuperFlash, MegaFlash, FlashGordon, FlashBeurk, etc., and all the little junk-gifts from *Mi-Microsoft-to-me*. And there you have it: the simple asshole becomes a genuine cyber-asshole.

**The Logic of Use vs. the Logic of the Master**

There is no need to recount yet again the glory of the origins of the Internet—that way in which a military invention was diverted first by researchers and then by a horde of spotty libertarians, their asses glued to their chairs. At its emergence into the public sphere, the Internet cultivated a low-tech aesthetic, one that still resonates today among all those who prefer the relevance of content and the intelligence of hypertext to the rhinestones and virtual glitter of push media and a web turned into bad television. Initially conceived as a system of free exchanges between individuals, beyond the control of any central authority, the Internet roughly followed the model of the telephone. But the telephone—let us not forget—was itself born of a détourning. Graham Bell, its inventor, had but one objective: to create prosthetic devices for the deaf-mute, just as Thomas Edison did with the phonograph.

Let us not forget this either: high tech perfectly symbolizes technical progress, which is supposed to nourish social and moral progress.

Early 1980s. France celebrates the birth of a new communication tool: the Minitel. Knowledge at the tip of the screen. To this delicious technological cake, a cherry is added to improve its decoration: mailbox-style messaging, allowing honest people to exchange information. Based in Grenoble, this miracle invention was intended for doctors. Alas\! Clever little rogues soon managed to worm their way onto this medical server and send each other torrid messages. In 1984, the Pink Minitel had just blossomed. Without even touching the cake prepared for them by institutions, citizens lunged straight for the cherry.

The logic of the technician and the inventor—or worse still, that of the master of technology in charge of selling it—is akin to the logic of high tech. Close to, or adapted to, the authoritarian taste of power, it proves directive, scholastic, very often administrative, messianic, universal, and of a quasi-divine order. It flatters in us a love of order, obedience, and the sense of Platonic perfection—pure and spotless, unstained by dubious liquors.

The logic of the user, who ignores all the myths, fantasies, and appetites of designers or masters of the machine, is akin to the logic of low tech. It appears unpredictable, hedonistic, and sometimes lazy. In the manner of Diogenes the Cynic. A play of masks and desires on 3615 Sexe, it chooses Dionysus over Prometheus.

**Trash Low Tech vs. Pop High Tech**

In music, the level of high technology can be measured by the place of recording. Low among the goldsmiths of the home studio, who make do with a sampler and a few old vinyl records lying around on their couch. High among those aspiring to pop Olympus, who inflate studio production to mask their lack of inspiration. Low with Photek and his brothers and sisters. High with the Spice Girls and their clones.

Today, aesthetics are arguably less high tech than they were fifteen or twenty-five years ago, in the eras of overproduced new wave or the monstrous virtuosity of progressive rock. Blame techno, which flirts with trash and claims the double pirate heritage of punk and acid house. Blame sampling, a low-tech recycling model. Blame precursors in organized noise chaos such as the Velvet Underground and later Suicide in New York, or Can and Neu\! in Germany in the early 1970s.

Diogenes, according to legend, had a passion for raw octopus. The musicians of Can love seafood. We are in Paris, around an immense breakfast spread. Can has not existed for nearly twenty years, and yet there they are, having fun, devouring oysters, shrimp, and spider crabs, and discussing the finer points around an album of covers of their past deliriums by today’s young techno-samurai.

Irmin Schmidt, former student of Stockhausen and anything but Platonic, explains:  
 “Our musical concept was that the environment in which we played took part in the compositional process. It was an idea close to sampling. Everything happening around us—a train in the background, for example—was included in the track during recording, the entire environment being a sample. And this idea stripped music of any excessive ambition and of any sacred character.”

And the stratospheric guitarist Michael Karoli adds, in a low-tech register:  
 “At the beginning, we had a simple tape recorder and two microphones, then a little later two tape recorders and two microphones\! Despite the almost terrorist consumerism that already reigned at the time, you could make music out of not much.”

Then Holger Czukay continues:  
 “At the beginning of the 1970s, bands wanted truly professional means of production—that’s why multitrack tape machines and large recording studios were born. Groups wanted a clean, perfect sound. Now look at DJs and musicians of the 1990s like Air Liquide: they buy rotten old electronic machines, hook them up anarchically, and then tell you, ‘Man, this is the beginning of a new music’ (laughs). And the guys from high-tech magazines, fans of Air Liquide in Germany, complain that these young artists don’t give a damn about their hyper-perfect machines.”

These techno musicians have reinvented the analog synthesizer. They no longer want to work with sequencers connected to a computer; they want a more direct, living relationship with the machine. It’s because of people like Air Liquide that Roland went back into producing analog synths. In the ’70s, people were looking for a very clean sound; these artists reinvent trash and impose a kind of new standard. It’s new—and funny.

**A Small Everyday Philosophy of Slow Tech**

The philosophy of Diogenes could be summed up in a single maxim: to be one’s own standard—a libertarian motto echoed by Lautréamont’s desire, taken up again in the 1960s by the Situationists Guy Debord and Raoul Vaneigem: that each person become the artist of their own life.

At its birth, every technological object comes with a standard. In 1981, Roland released the TB-303 drum machine so that musicians could easily produce jazz and funk bass sounds. Two years later, Chris & Cosey—an electronic duo emerging from the industrial masters Throbbing Gristle—hijacked the TB-303 to embody, through obsessive and overdriven loops, the ghosts of their track *“Dancing Ghosts.”* Like the corsairs of acid house who would follow, the two musicians made themselves their own standard. In defiance of high-tech manuals, they listened only to their imagination.

When you drive calmly down the roads in a patched-up old wreck, bought second-hand **without even a glance at television ads, you are low tech in complete thrift.** Without even thinking about it—and all the better for it. When you keep typing your texts on your good old Mac SE 30, using your little sister’s Word 5.0, not giving a damn about the Windows PC circus or the Word 7.0 that Papa Bill dangles under your nose, you are low tech in total laziness. Without even thinking about it—and all the better for it.

When you organize an improvised rave according to the art of sound systems born in Jamaica in the early 1950s, using grandma’s turntable, an amp, and speaker cabinets lovingly cobbled together without any regard for hi-fi standards, you are low tech in pure jubilation. Without even thinking about it—and all the better for it.

Take a stick and draw in the sand. Grab your pencil and tap out a rhythm on your lamp. Reinvent the computer with sheets of paper. Use *Crash* as a hat if it rains. Sample the world and make it your own. Low tech, in reality, does not exist—because the tool does not matter.

Be Dada. Be pirates. Be cynical philosophers. And don’t listen to the lesson-givers. Don’t listen to me.
